# Understanding Reactivity and Aggression, Part 1

Narration script. About 2,732 words, roughly 18 minutes at a relaxed pace.

## Aggression, reactivity and function

Let's start with two words you'll hear all through this course: aggression and reactivity. They're not the same thing, but they overlap. Aggression is behavior that threatens, pushes away, controls, protects, or causes harm. It can show up in very different emotional situations. Reactivity is an intense, over-the-top response to something in the environment. Think of a smoke alarm that screams every time you make toast. A reactive response can include aggressive behavior, but it doesn't have to. And aggressive behavior alone doesn't tell us what the dog is feeling, or what the behavior is doing for them. That's why the label is only the starting point.

A dog may bark, growl, lunge, snap, freeze, move away, or bite. Those behaviors matter, but they don't tell the whole story. Behavior is information. When someone stands too close in line, you don't shove them. You shift, give them a look, maybe say excuse me. Dogs do the same, with a hard stare, a stiff body, or a growl. But behavior isn't a direct translation of emotion. The same bark could mean go away. It could mean come closer. It could mean give me that. Or it could be arousal boiling over. Two dogs can do the same thing for different reasons. And the same dog can do it for different reasons in different situations. Context matters. So we look at what happened before the behavior, what the dog did, and what happened after.

One of the ways we look at behavior in context is with the ABCs. A is the antecedent, what happens right before. B is the behavior, what your dog does. C is the consequence, what happens right after. Look at all three, and you start to see what's happening around the behavior, and what it may be doing for your dog.

Let's look at an example. A: a person appears outside the window. B: your dog launches at the window and barks. C: the person comes inside and gives your dog attention. So the barking may be helping bring the person closer.

Now a different situation. A: you're out on a walk, and a person heads your way. B: your dog plants their feet and barks. It can look protective, but it's often fear. C: the person stops, or moves away. Same kind of behavior, but a different consequence. This time, the barking got more distance.

Now a dog with a toy. A: another dog walks over. B: your dog gets up, growls, and snaps. C: the other dog backs away, and your dog keeps the toy. Here the behavior may be keeping access to something valuable.

Notice something important. These behaviors can look alike. One dog barks to bring someone closer. Another barks to make someone leave. Another growls and snaps to keep a toy. Treat them all the same, and you'd get it wrong. Inviting the person closer would thrill one dog and push another right over the edge. The behavior alone doesn't tell us its function. The ABC gives us the context to figure it out.

Once we have the ABC, we can ask: what did the behavior accomplish? That's what we mean by function. Function isn't what the behavior looks like. It's what the behavior does in that situation. A behavior can change the environment, change what someone else does, get the dog access to something, or change how the dog feels.

There are four broad directions a function can go. Increase distance. Decrease distance. Gain or keep access. Or release built-up tension. These aren't rigid boxes. A behavior can do more than one job, and the job can change with the situation.

Increase distance means more space between the dog and something in the environment. The dog barks, growls, lunges, or moves away, and the person or dog backs off. To a scared dog, that space is everything. Decrease distance works the opposite way. The behavior brings the dog closer to something they want: a person, another dog, an object, or an activity. And when a leash stops them getting there, it's like road rage. You can see exactly where you want to go, and you're stuck in traffic. Sooner or later, you lean on the horn.

Sometimes it's not about distance. The behavior helps the dog get something, or keep something they already have: food, a toy, a person, a resting spot. Our toy guarder is a good example. Growl, snap, the other dog backs off, and the toy stays put. Then there's release. When arousal gets too high to manage, a dog may bark, jump, grab, spin, mouth, or redirect onto something else. Think of the dog who spins and screams when you grab the leash. Release doesn't tell us which emotion started it. It tells us the behavior may be helping the dog discharge an intense state.

One catch: which way a dog moves doesn't tell you the function. A fearful dog may lunge toward another dog. It looks like they want to get closer. But if the other dog moves away, the lunge worked to create distance. The dog moved toward. The outcome was more space. That's why we look at the whole ABC, not just the direction the dog moves.

Function and emotion are related, but they're not the same. Function is what the behavior accomplished. Emotion is what the dog may be feeling. A fearful dog may use behavior to get distance. A frustrated dog, to get access. An excited dog, to get closer. Think of an iceberg. The behavior is the tip we see. Fear, frustration, excitement and conflict sit below the waterline. But one emotion doesn't always make one behavior. One fearful dog freezes. Another runs. Another barks and lunges. And the same behavior can come from different emotions. So the behavior alone isn't an emotional diagnosis. We look at the whole picture.

Once we've looked at the ABC and the function, we can ask: why might this dog respond this way? And right behind that, most owners ask, what did I do wrong? The honest answer? It's almost never one thing, or one mistake. It works like a recipe: several ingredients mix together, and the same ingredients can cook up reactivity, aggression, or both.

Ingredient one, genetics. Some dogs are simply born more sensitive, with a touchier smoke alarm from day one. Breed traits nudge things too. Herders were made to chase and control movement, terriers to hunt, guardians to watch over home. Breed isn't destiny, but it loads the dice. Those drives don't switch off. Without a healthy outlet, the pressure builds, and a bike or the mail carrier becomes the target. Flirt poles, sniffing games or tug give that drive a job. Ingredient two, socialization. In roughly the first three months, a puppy's brain sorts the world into safe and scary. What they meet calmly in that window tends to feel normal later. Miss that window, and buses, strangers and skateboards can stay harder to file as normal. If you adopted an older dog, it closed before you met. Harder, not hopeless. Or the other way: too much, too fast, and a puppy learns the world is overwhelming. And a puppy who greeted every dog learns every dog means a party, until the leash says no. Hello, frustration. If that was your puppy, you followed common advice.

Ingredient three, age. Adolescence runs from about six to eighteen months, up to two years in some big breeds. Think teenager with fur: big feelings, less self-control. Then comes social maturity, when your dog becomes an adult, between about one and three years. This is when reactivity or aggression often shows up or gets louder. Many dogs also get pickier about other dogs as they grow up. It's like a teenager who wants to hit the club and make a ton of friends. As an adult, they'd rather skip the club and see a few close friends. That's normal, not a problem. But the dog who loved the dog park at one may not want it at three, and pushing them can end in a scuffle. Senior dogs change too. Sore joints lower tolerance, and fading senses or a foggy memory make the world less predictable, and less safe.

Ingredient four, past experiences. One scary moment can be enough: if an off-leash dog rushes yours once, they may never forget it. Fear learns fast. Ingredient five, training tools. Research links harsh methods with more fear, stress and aggression. Think leash jerks, prong, choke or shock collars, and pinning a dog down. If your dog feels pain whenever another dog appears, they learn other dogs predict pain. The fear grows, even if the barking stops for a while. Those tools often come from the outdated idea that your dog wants to be the boss. Dogs who react or aggress aren't after rank. They're scared, frustrated, overwhelmed, or protecting something they value. If you've used any of these, be kind to yourself. You did your best, and now you know more. A trainer who works without force can help you switch safely to a comfortable harness.

Ingredient six, barriers. Remember road rage? A leash, fence or window is the traffic jam: your dog sees what they want, can't reach it, and the pressure climbs. It's how a lot of fence fighting starts. Ingredient seven, pain, and don't skip this one. Think how short your fuse gets with a headache. Dogs feel that too, and hide it well. Pain is common in dogs with behavior problems, and it's a big reason behind sudden growling or snapping. So every dog with reactivity or aggression needs a vet check, especially after a sudden change. And there are more: learning history, the environment, and how aroused your dog already is. We'll get into those later in the course. Please hear this. Most of this list was never in your control, and none of it is your fault. But understanding it is now in your hands.

Now back to the consequence. What happens after a behavior changes what happens next time. Say barking makes something scary go away. That relief is a powerful reward, so barking gets more likely next time. The same goes for barking that brings someone closer, or a snap that keeps another dog off the toy. The behavior worked. And behavior that works gets repeated. Every time the loop runs, it gets faster and stronger. Your dog reacts sooner, and from farther away. It's like a path across a lawn: walk it every day and it wears in, until it's the way you go without thinking. The good news is we can build a new path.

When we talk about reinforcement, we're not only talking about treats. The world reinforces behavior too. More distance can reinforce a behavior. So can access, attention, and relief. Your dog doesn't need to understand why it worked. The outcome alone can change what happens next time.

Now let's take everything we've learned and apply it to something we see all the time: a dog on leash who wants space. First, before training. A: another dog gets too close, too fast. The smoke alarm goes off. B: barking, growling, lunging. The full show, right there on the path. C: you create space, and your dog settles. Huge relief. Before training, this pattern repeats again and again. From your dog's point of view, the behavior worked, and every repeat is more practice. Don't try to fix it by standing your ground. That just traps your dog in the scary moment. Leaving is still right. We just need a better way to get there.

When training starts, one of the first things we change is the A, the antecedent. We work with the function, not against it: your dog still gets space. Instead of letting your dog get too close again and again, we change the setup. More distance, a manageable place, better timing, and less rehearsal. And we watch your dog and adjust before they're overwhelmed. A dog who's calm enough to think can learn. Try this on your next walk: spot the other dog before your dog does. That head start lets you turn around before the alarm goes off.

Then we change the B. Instead of barking, growling and lunging, your dog learns something else to do: looking at you, checking in, or moving with you. The goal isn't just to stop the old behavior. We're giving your dog something else to do. But that's not enough on its own. If your dog needs space, the new behavior has to get it. We can't teach them that staying calm means staying trapped next to the trigger. So we keep the C meaningful: space, relief, safety. We change the behavior that gets the outcome, not the dog's need for it. It's like a toddler who melts down when they need a break. You don't cancel the break. You teach them to say, I need a break. So now calm is what works, and calm is what grows.

With practice, the pattern changes. The trigger appears, your dog notices, and instead of barking and lunging, they use the new behavior. You respond, and your dog gets what they need: space, safety, relief. The ABC has changed. Not a dog who never feels anything, but a dog with a better plan. You'll see it in small moments first, like a faster recovery or a glance back at you. Celebrate those.

This is why function matters. If we don't know what a behavior is accomplishing, we can build a plan that takes away something the dog needs. If the behavior creates distance, we address distance. If it's about access, we address access. If it's arousal, we help the dog learn to regulate it. The goal isn't just to make barking, growling or lunging disappear. We understand why it happens, change the conditions, teach a new response, and make sure it still works for the dog. We never take away what your dog needs. We give them a better way to ask for it.

The same framework helps us read warning signals. Many dogs climb a ladder of warnings before a bite, though a startled or hurting dog can race up it in a split second. The bottom rungs are quiet: turning away, freezing, stiffening, a hard stare. If nobody listens, it gets louder. A growl, a lip lift, a snap. And at the very top, a bite. Not every dog shows every rung. Some climb fast, and some signals are subtle and easy to miss. Every rung below the bite is information. The earlier you notice a change, the more options you have.

That's why we don't punish warnings. If you have, you're in good company. Most of us were taught to. Punishing a growl is like taping over your car's check engine light. The light's gone, but the engine problem isn't. You've just lost your warning. Punish a growl, and you may stop the growl without changing what made your dog feel they needed to warn. They're still uncomfortable. You just have less information. That's how you get a dog who seems to bite out of nowhere. A growl isn't the problem to punish. It's information to understand. So stop, create space, and look at the ABC.

So here's your homework. Grab a notebook and start a list of the situations where your dog reacts, so together we can map out the ABC for each one. Get specific. Not just dogs, but which dogs, how close, where, when, and how big the reaction was, from one to ten. Something like: big dogs, head-on, after dark. And count quiet reactions too, like a freeze or a sudden stop in sniffing. Then write down what happened right before, what your dog did, and what happened right after. That's your A, B and C, and it's where we'll start.

So when you see behavior that worries you, start with the ABC. What happened before? What did your dog do? What happened after? Then ask: what did the behavior accomplish? And why might your dog be responding this way? Aggression and reactivity look different in every dog. The goal isn't to memorize a label. It's to see the whole pattern. Understanding comes first. Then we can change behavior. I'll see you in the next lesson.
