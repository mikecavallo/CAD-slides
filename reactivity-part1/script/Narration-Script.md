# Understanding Reactivity and Aggression, Part 1

Narration script. About 3,943 words, roughly 26 minutes at a relaxed pace.

## Welcome

Picture a quiet morning walk. Your dog's sniffing along, life is good. Then you see it: another dog, turning the corner up ahead. Your stomach drops. Your hand tightens on the leash. A second later, your sweet couch potato is barking, lunging and spinning like a dog you've never met. People stare. And you cross the street with your face on fire, wondering what you did wrong.

If that sounds like your life, I want you to hear this first. You're not a bad owner. And you don't have a bad dog. You have a dog who's having a hard time. And that's something we can work on together. Welcome to Part One. Before we change anything, let's understand what's going on inside your dog. By the end, you'll understand three things: what your dog is feeling, what the behavior does for them, and why some days are worse. The feeling, the function and the full cup.

## What is aggression?

First, the word that scares owners most: aggression. It may never fit your dog, but you'll hear it everywhere, so let's get it right. Aggression isn't a label for who your dog is. It describes what a behavior is for, usually one of four jobs. Job one, create distance. Get away from me, or make that thing go away. Job two, stop a perceived threat. Notice that word, perceived. The threat only has to feel dangerous to your dog. Job three, guard something they value, like a bone or the couch. Job four, change how things turn out, like growling so the brushing stops.

When someone stands too close in line, you don't shove them. You shift, give them a look, maybe say excuse me. Dogs do the same. A hard stare, a growl, a snap: each says back off, go away, or this is mine. Even a bite is a message, just the loudest. Under every message is a feeling: usually fear, sometimes frustration, sometimes a strong urge to guard. The behavior is the part we see. What makes it aggression? Your dog is actively trying to make something happen. That's behavior with intent: not spite, just a goal.

Luckily, most dogs don't go straight to a bite. They climb a ladder of warnings first, though a startled or hurting dog can race up it in a split second. The bottom rungs are quiet: a lip lick, a yawn that doesn't fit the moment, a head turned away. Then the body stiffens and the stare goes hard. If nobody listens, it gets louder. A growl, a lip lifted to show teeth, then a snap, often at the air. Even here, your dog is holding back. And only at the very top, after all of that, comes a bite. Every rung below the bite is your dog trying not to bite. Those warnings are a gift. Barking and lunging can be loud warnings too. This week, watch for the quiet rungs first, like a lip lick, a look away or a stiff body, and give your dog space.

## What is reactivity?

Next, the word that probably fits your dog: reactivity. It's an over-the-top emotional reaction to something in your dog's world, way bigger than the moment calls for. Picture a smoke alarm that screams every time you make toast. The alarm isn't broken. It's set way too sensitive. You'll see it as barking, lunging, whining or pacing. Big feelings, out in the open.

Here's the big idea. Think of reactivity as the volume on that smoke alarm. It tells you how loud, not why. Take two dogs barking at the same mail carrier, both at max. One wants the mail carrier gone. The other is desperate to say hi. Same volume, different reason. And the reason is where the useful information is.

Direction one is away: your dog is scared and wants distance. A skateboard rattles by, and they bark like their life depends on it. To them, it kind of does. Direction two is toward. Your dog wants something so badly it hurts, like the dog across the street or a squirrel. And the leash says no. That's road rage. You can see exactly where you want to go, and you're stuck in traffic. Sooner or later, you lean on the horn. Direction three is release: built-up tension or excitement bursting out, and sometimes it's even fun. Think of the dog who spins and screams when you grab the leash. One catch: scared dogs often lunge toward what they fear, to drive it off. So don't judge by the pull. Ask what they're trying to get. Lots of dogs mix all three. Next time yours goes off, ask: away, toward, or release? Guessing is fine.

## Reactive or aggressive?

What's the difference between a reactive dog and an aggressive dog? From across the street, they can look exactly the same. Reactivity is about intensity, a wave of feeling that spills over. Aggression is about intent: using behavior to get a result. The reactive dog is saying, this is too much. The dog using aggression is saying, I'll make this stop. In real life there's a lot of overlap. A reactive dog's barking often gets results too, so many dogs are a bit of both.

The truth is, a reactive dog can become aggressive. Notice I said can, not will. Many reactive dogs never get there, and it's rarely overnight. It happens in four common ways, and once you see them, you can get ahead of them.

One, the behavior works. Your dog barks and lunges, and the other dog walks away. It was leaving anyway, but the barking gets the credit. And that relief is a powerful reward. Behavior that works gets repeated, and that's how a burst of feeling slowly becomes a strategy your dog reaches for. Two, practice. Every time that loop runs, it gets faster and stronger. Your dog reacts sooner, and from farther away. It's like a path across a lawn: walk it every day and it wears in, until it's the way you go without thinking. Your dog's brain works the same way, and the good news is we can build a new path.

Three, the warnings stop working. If nobody listens to a growl, or someone punishes it, your dog learns the polite rungs are pointless. So next time, they may skip the warnings and go straight to the top. Four, frustration boils over. A dog who's held back and wound up can whip around and snap at whatever's closest, even your hand. That's redirection: not spite, just pressure with nowhere to go. So when your dog's wound up, keep your hands clear of their collar and face, and add distance.

Which brings me to the most important rule here: never punish a growl. If you have, you're in good company. Most of us were taught to. Punishing a growl is like taping over your car's check engine light. The light's gone, but the engine problem isn't. You've just lost your warning. Punishment can stop the growl, but not the fear underneath, and often makes the fear worse. That's how you get a dog who seems to bite out of nowhere. It wasn't out of nowhere. Either punishment erased the warning, or the warning was too quiet to notice. So when your dog growls, thank them: stop, give space, and later, note what came before.

## Why it happens

The question I hear most is, why is my dog like this? And right behind it, what did I do wrong? The honest answer? It's almost never one thing, or one mistake. It's a recipe, and here are seven common ingredients.

Ingredient one, genetics. Some dogs are simply born more sensitive, with a touchier smoke alarm from day one. Breed traits nudge things too. Herders were bred to watch movement, guardians to distrust strangers. Breed isn't destiny, but those jobs don't switch off on a sidewalk. Ingredient two, socialization. In roughly the first three months, a puppy's brain sorts the world into safe and scary. What they meet calmly in that window tends to feel normal later. Miss that window, and buses, strangers and skateboards can stay harder to file as normal. If you adopted an older dog, it closed before you met. Harder, not hopeless. Or the other way: too much, too fast, and a puppy learns the world is overwhelming. And a puppy who greeted every dog learns every dog means a party, until the leash says no. Hello, frustration. If that was your puppy, you followed common advice.

Ingredient three, age. Adolescence runs from about six to eighteen months, up to two years in some big breeds. Think teenager with fur: big feelings, less self-control. Then comes social maturity, when your dog becomes an adult, between about one and three years. Reactivity often shows up or gets louder here. If your sweet puppy changed around age two, that may be why. Senior dogs change too. Sore joints lower tolerance, and fading senses or a foggy memory make the world less predictable, and less safe.

Ingredient four, past experiences. One scary moment can be enough: if an off-leash dog rushes yours once, they may never forget it. Fear learns fast. Ingredient five, training tools. Research links harsh methods with more fear, stress and aggression. Think leash jerks, prong, choke or shock collars, and pinning a dog down. If your dog feels pain whenever another dog appears, they learn other dogs predict pain. The fear grows, even if the barking stops for a while. Those tools often come from the outdated idea that your dog wants to be the boss. Reactive dogs aren't after rank. They're scared, frustrated or overwhelmed. If you've used any of these, be kind to yourself. You did your best, and now you know more. A trainer who works without force can help you switch safely to a comfortable harness.

Ingredient six, barriers. Remember road rage? A leash, fence or window is the traffic jam: your dog sees what they want, can't reach it, and the pressure climbs. Ingredient seven, pain, and don't skip this one. Think how short your fuse gets with a headache. Dogs feel that too, and hide it well. Pain is common in dogs with behavior problems, and it doesn't always look like limping. So every reactive dog needs a vet check, especially after a sudden change. And there are many other factors, which we'll get into later in the course. Please hear this. Most of this list was never in your control, and none of it is your fault. But understanding it is now in your hands.

## What's the function?

Now, why does it keep happening? Ask yourself: what job is this behavior doing for my dog? That job is its function. Remember, behavior that works gets repeated. Trainers call that reinforcement: something that happens right after a behavior and makes it more likely to happen again. To see it, I use the A, B, C. A is the antecedent, what happened right before. B is the behavior, what your dog did. And C is the consequence, what happened right after. Feelings start a reaction, and consequences help decide whether it comes back, so we'll work on both.

Let's try one. A, the antecedent: someone shows up outside your front window. B, your dog launches at the window, barking like the house is on fire. C, the person comes in, and your dog gets a big hello. As far as they can tell, barking opened the door. Something good got added, so the barking grows. That's positive reinforcement, and positive just means added.

Different dog, different story. A, you're out on a walk, and a stranger heads your way. B, your dog plants their feet in front of you and barks. Deep, serious barking. It can look protective, but it's often fear. C, the person walks away. To your dog, barking made the stranger leave. It worked too. This time something unpleasant got taken away. That's negative reinforcement. Negative doesn't mean bad, it means removed, and it's just as powerful.

Side by side: same barking, opposite jobs. One dog wants people closer, the other wants them gone. Treat them the same, and you'd get it wrong. Inviting the person closer would thrill one dog and push the other right up the warning ladder. That's why naming the A, B and C comes first. Once you know the job, you can teach a better way to do it. So grab a notebook. Pick one moment this week and write down all three, just the facts, like a reporter.

## The reactive walk: before, during and after training

Let's run the A, B, C on the walk that probably brought you here, before training. A: suddenly there's another dog, too close and too fast. The smoke alarm goes off. B, barking, lunging, growling. The full show, right there on the path, with an audience. C, you hurry away, or the other person crosses the street. Your dog gets space and huge relief. And there's the trap. Your dog wanted space, and barking got it. That's negative reinforcement, so next time, barking is even more likely. Don't try to stop the payoff by standing your ground. That just traps your dog in the scary moment. Leaving is still right, and we'll teach a calmer way to ask for space.

So how do we change it? Change the A, change the B, keep the C. First, the A: management. More distance, quieter routes, calmer times of day, and turning around early, so fewer things surprise your dog. Now your dog isn't surprised or overwhelmed. They're calm enough to think, and a dog who can think can learn. Try this on your next walk: spot the other dog before your dog does. That head start lets you turn around before the alarm goes off.

Next, the B. We teach your dog something new to do when that other dog shows up. We don't punish barking, growling or lunging away. We just make them unnecessary. Instead, your dog learns check-ins, where they glance up at you, plus loose-leash walking and leave it, which means turning away on cue. And the C? We keep it the same: space and relief. Now it comes after the calm behavior, not the barking. It's like a toddler who melts down when they need a break. You don't cancel the break. You teach them to say, I need a break. So now calm is what pays off, and calm is what grows.

And after training? You need less management, and your dog reaches for the new behavior on their own. It still ends with space. Two dogs pass on the path, and nothing happens. Not a dog who never feels anything, but a dog with a better plan. We never took away what your dog needed. We gave them a better way to ask for it. You'll see that after picture in small moments first, like a faster recovery or a glance back at you. Celebrate those.

## What is a trigger?

The dog on the corner, the skateboard, the mail carrier: these are triggers. A trigger is anything that raises your dog's stress, whether they react out loud, quietly, or not at all. That last part matters. A dog who freezes and stares can be just as stressed as a barking dog. Sometimes more.

Inside your dog, it goes like this. The trigger causes an emotion first, and that emotion drives the reaction. You know that jolt when a friend jumps out and yells boo? Your heart leaps before you know it's them. Feeling first, scream second. That's why we work on the feeling, not just the barking. Change the feeling, and the reaction changes with it.

Every dog has their own list. Some are fine with every person on the planet, until one is wearing a hat. As I sort them, count how many are on your dog's list. People, like strangers, joggers and running kids. Dogs and other animals, like cats and squirrels. Then sounds, like the doorbell and fireworks. And moving things, like bikes, skateboards and buses. Then handling, like nail trims or being touched. And places and situations, like the vet or being left alone.

Maybe someone told you to keep exposing your dog to the scary thing until they get over it. That can make things worse. If you're scared of spiders, a night in a room full of them won't make you love them. You're just surviving. That's flooding. Flooding can cause sensitization, where fear grows with every exposure. Daily walks that get too close can do it too. Or a dog shuts down and goes quiet, which looks calm but isn't. A dog only gets used to something when the dose is small enough to feel safe. Far enough away, quiet enough, and short enough.

A puzzle: on Monday your dog ignores the jogger. On Thursday, the same jogger sets off a meltdown. That's not random. For now, grab that notebook and start your dog's trigger list, right next to your A, B, C notes. You can't change what you haven't named. And get specific. Not just dogs, but which dogs, how close, where, when, and how big the reaction was, from one to ten. Something like: big dogs, head-on, after dark. And count quiet reactions too, like a freeze or a sudden stop in sniffing.

## Stress and recovery over time

So why is Monday fine and Thursday a disaster? Three ideas: threshold, stress stacking up, and how slowly stress drains away. First idea, threshold: the line between coping and not coping. Under it, your dog can notice the trigger and still think: hear you, take a treat, make choices. Over it, the thinking brain goes offline and big feelings grab the wheel. That's fight, flight or freeze for a scared dog, and road rage for a frustrated one. Imagine doing long division while a bear chases you down the street. You're not being stubborn. That part of your brain isn't available. That's why a sit falls apart mid-lunge. Your dog isn't ignoring you, they can't take it in. Training happens under the line.

How can you tell your dog is getting close? They stop taking food, or suddenly snatch it, and they stop hearing their name. Their eyes lock on and won't let go. The body goes tense, the mouth closes tight, and their weight shifts forward. They start to pull. Or their head's on a swivel, scanning everything for the next thing. When you see these, don't wait for the explosion. Add distance right away, because staying under the line beats coming back down. Jot down how far away the trigger was at that first sign. It shifts day to day, so never go hunting for triggers to test it.

The second idea: stress stacks up. Think of a cup, and every stressful thing pours a little in. The doorbell at breakfast, pour. The garbage truck roaring past, pour. A skateboard rattling by on your walk, and the cup fills higher. Then comes the jogger. On a calm day, no big deal. But today the cup was nearly full, so it tips over the line. It wasn't only the jogger. It was a full cup, including things you can't see, like poor sleep or a sore hip. That's trigger stacking, and that's your Thursday. On a graph, each trigger makes stress spike, and before it can drift back down, the next one lands on top. Each spike starts higher, until the jogger tips it over. From outside, it looks like a meltdown over one jogger. From inside, it was the whole day. It can stack all at once, too. A dog, a skateboard and a shouting kid in the same ten seconds.

And the third idea, the part most people never hear: stress goes up fast, but comes down slowly. Remember that friend who yelled boo? You were laughing a second later. But your heart kept pounding for a good while after. Same for your dog. Adrenaline hits in seconds, but cortisol, a slower stress hormone, lingers. A big scare can take hours, sometimes days, to settle. So yesterday's scare can still be in the cup on today's walk. If Wednesday was a vet visit, Thursday starts half full before you leave the house.

After a big scare or a busy day, plan a decompression day to drain the cup: sniffing games, chews, food puzzles, naps. And yes, skipping the walk is okay. And don't stack hard days back to back. If Saturday is grooming, Sunday isn't the day for the street fair. An emptier cup means more room before the line, and more chances for your dog to succeed. Before every walk, ask yourself one question: what's already in my dog's cup today?

## Your key takeaways

Let's pull it all together. Six things I want you to take with you. One, reactivity is an emotional response, not disobedience. Your dog isn't being stubborn. Their smoke alarm is going off. Two, read the threshold. Under the line, your dog can learn what you're teaching. Over it, they can only practice the reaction. Three, every dog has their own list of triggers. Get specific. And if you only do one thing this week, start that list.

Four, stress stacks up, over time or all at once. When a walk goes wrong, look at the whole day, and the day before. Five, aggression is communication, so never punish a growl. Don't tape over the warning light. Thank it, give space, and go fix the engine. And six. If there are bites, if things are escalating, or if you're scared of your own dog, you don't have to do this alone. Work with a force-free pro, someone who trains without pain or fear, and have your vet rule out pain. Some dogs also need medication from your vet or a veterinary behaviorist, a vet who specializes in behavior. That's not failure. Meanwhile, keep everyone safe with gates and supervision. Never leave kids alone with any dog, especially one who's warned or bitten. And ask your pro about muzzle training, done kindly.

## Where to go from here

Understanding the why is where it all starts, and you've just done it. Changing it takes three steps. Step one, manage first, so everyone stays safe and your dog stops rehearsing. Remember that lawn? Management keeps the old path from getting deeper while we build a new one. Step two, change the emotion, because feelings drive the behavior. We use two tools together. Counterconditioning means every time the trigger appears, something wonderful follows, usually great food, until the trigger itself feels like good news. And no, you're not rewarding barking. At the right distance, there isn't any. Desensitization means starting so far away, or so quietly, that your dog stays relaxed, then building up bit by bit. It's the opposite of flooding. Step three, your situation, with practical setups for walks, visitors, guarding, and dogs who clash with other dogs.

With the right plan, reactivity can get a whole lot better. I won't call it a cure, and some dogs will always need a little management. That's okay. It won't be a straight line. You'll have great weeks and rough days, and now you know why. We're after progress, not perfection. Think back to that first walk. Next time a dog turns that corner and yours goes off, you won't see a bad dog. You'll see a feeling, a function, and maybe a full cup. Your dog isn't giving you a hard time. They're having a hard time. And now you know how to start helping. Thank you for being the kind of owner who wants to understand. See you in the next lesson, here at Calling All Dogs.
