Chapter 1: Aggression, reactivity and function

ch01s01: Aggression and reactivity

Let's start with two words you'll hear all through this course: aggression and reactivity. They're not the same thing, but they overlap.

On screen: Text: “Aggression and reactivity” · The heading 'Aggression and reactivity' writes on in green; the snarling dog photo and the barking white dog photo slide in as two large circles side by side, labeled 'Aggression' and 'Reactivity'.

Aggression is behavior that threatens, pushes away, controls, protects, or causes harm. It can show up in very different emotional situations.

On screen: Under the aggression circle, five small chips pop in one per word: Threaten, Push away, Control, Protect, Harm.

Reactivity is an intense, over-the-top response to something in the environment. Think of a smoke alarm that screams every time you make toast.

On screen: Under the reactivity circle, a smoke alarm icon appears beside a tiny toast icon; red sound rings swell huge while the toast stays small.

A reactive response can include aggressive behavior, but it doesn't have to. And aggressive behavior alone doesn't tell us what the dog is feeling, or what the behavior is doing for them.

On screen: Text: “They overlap, but they're not the same” · The chips and alarm fade; the two circles slide together into a Venn diagram and the overlap tints light green.

That's why the label is only the starting point.

On screen: Text: “The label is only the starting point” · A 'Label' tag lands on the overlap and a green arrow runs from it to a card: 'The starting point, not the whole story'.

ch01s02: Look beyond the label

A dog may bark, growl, lunge, snap, freeze, move away, or bite. Those behaviors matter, but they don't tell the whole story.

On screen: Text: “Look beyond the label” · The heading 'Look beyond the label' writes on and seven behavior chips pop in a row, one per word: Bark, Growl, Lunge, Snap, Freeze, Move away, Bite.

Behavior is information. When someone stands too close in line, you don't shove them. You shift, give them a look, maybe say excuse me. Dogs do the same, with a hard stare, a stiff body, or a growl.

On screen: Text: “Behavior is information” · The chips shrink up; two person icons stand side by side, one inching closer, and a bubble reading 'Excuse me.' pops from the other. Then the person swaps for a dog icon with small tags: Hard stare, Stiff body, Growl.

But behavior isn't a direct translation of emotion. The same bark could mean go away. It could mean come closer. It could mean give me that. Or it could be arousal boiling over.

On screen: Text: “Same bark, different meanings” · A single 'Bark' chip sits at center and four speech bubbles pop around it, one per phrase: 'Go away', 'Come closer', 'Give me that', 'Too much energy'.

Two dogs can do the same thing for different reasons. And the same dog can do it for different reasons in different situations. Context matters.

On screen: Text: “Context matters” · The bubbles fade; two dog icons appear with identical 'Bark' chips, one with a small alert-triangle above it and one with a heart.

So we look at what happened before the behavior, what the dog did, and what happened after.

On screen: Text: “Before. Behavior. After.” · Three empty boxes slide in left to right with arrows between them: 'Before', 'Behavior', 'After'.

ch01s03: The ABCs

One of the ways we look at behavior in context is with the ABCs.

ch01s07: Same behavior, different job

Notice something important. These behaviors can look alike. One dog barks to bring someone closer. Another barks to make someone leave. Another growls and snaps to keep a toy.

Treat them all the same, and you'd get it wrong. Inviting the person closer would thrill one dog and push another right over the edge.

The behavior alone doesn't tell us its function. The ABC gives us the context to figure it out.

ch01s08: What's the function?

Once we have the ABC, we can ask: what did the behavior accomplish? That's what we mean by function.

On screen: Text: “What did the behavior accomplish?” · The heading 'Function' writes on large in green with a target icon, and 'What did the behavior accomplish?' fades in beneath it.

Function isn't what the behavior looks like. It's what the behavior does in that situation.

On screen: Text: “Not how it looks. What it does.” · Two cards slide up: an eye icon card 'What it looks like' that greys out, and a green target card 'What it does'.

A behavior can change the environment, change what someone else does, get the dog access to something, or change how the dog feels.

On screen: Four chips pop in one per phrase: 'Changes the environment', 'Changes what others do', 'Gets access', 'Changes how the dog feels'.

ch01s09: Four directions

There are four broad directions a function can go. Increase distance. Decrease distance. Gain or keep access. Or release built-up tension.

On screen: Text: “Four functional directions” · A round dog badge appears at center and four arrows fan out one per phrase: left 'Increase distance', right 'Decrease distance', down 'Gain or keep access', up 'Release'.

These aren't rigid boxes. A behavior can do more than one job, and the job can change with the situation.

On screen: Text: “Not rigid boxes” · All four arrows glow together and a dotted ring draws around them, joining them.

ch01s10: Increase or decrease distance

Increase distance means more space between the dog and something in the environment. The dog barks, growls, lunges, or moves away, and the person or dog backs off. To a scared dog, that space is everything.

On screen: Text: “Increase distance” · The hub shrinks left with its left arrow lit; a dog icon and a trigger icon sit apart with a distance bar between them that stretches wider, labeled 'More space'.

Decrease distance works the opposite way. The behavior brings the dog closer to something they want: a person, another dog, an object, or an activity.

On screen: Text: “Decrease distance” · The right arrow lights; the trigger icon swaps for a heart icon and the bar shrinks, labeled 'Less space'.

And when a leash stops them getting there, it's like road rage. You can see exactly where you want to go, and you're stuck in traffic. Sooner or later, you lean on the horn.

On screen: Text: “Leash road rage” · A car icon slides in on the bar with horn sound lines bursting from it, and a leash line pulls taut.

ch01s11: Access and release

Sometimes it's not about distance. The behavior helps the dog get something, or keep something they already have: food, a toy, a person, a resting spot.

On screen: Text: “Gain or keep access” · The hub's down arrow lights; two cards appear: 'Get it.' and 'Keep it.', with small bone, toy, person and bed icons.

Our toy guarder is a good example. Growl, snap, the other dog backs off, and the toy stays put.

On screen: the guard C illustration slides in as a small card beside 'Keep it.' with a green check.

Then there's release. When arousal gets too high to manage, a dog may bark, jump, grab, spin, mouth, or redirect onto something else. Think of the dog who spins and screams when you grab the leash.

On screen: Text: “Release built-up tension” · The cards clear; the up arrow bursts with a zap icon and chips pop in: Bark, Jump, Grab, Spin, Mouth, Redirect.

Release doesn't tell us which emotion started it. It tells us the behavior may be helping the dog discharge an intense state.

On screen: Text: “High arousal, behavior, change in state” · The chips gather into a strip: 'High arousal', arrow, 'Behavior', arrow, 'Change in state'.

ch01s12: Movement isn't function

One catch: which way a dog moves doesn't tell you the function. A fearful dog may lunge toward another dog. It looks like they want to get closer.

On screen: Text: “Movement isn't function” · Two dog icons on a path; the left dog lunges toward the right one along a green arrow labeled 'Lunges toward'.

But if the other dog moves away, the lunge worked to create distance. The dog moved toward. The outcome was more space.

On screen: Text: “Toward, but the result is space” · The right dog slides away and a distance bar stretches between them, labeled 'More distance'.

That's why we look at the whole ABC, not just the direction the dog moves.

On screen: Text: “Watch the outcome, not the pull” · A small A, B, C strip slides in beneath: 'Dog lunges toward', 'Other dog moves away', 'More distance'.

ch01s13: Function and emotion

Function and emotion are related, but they're not the same. Function is what the behavior accomplished. Emotion is what the dog may be feeling.

A fearful dog may use behavior to get distance. A frustrated dog, to get access. An excited dog, to get closer.

On screen: Three rows tick in one per phrase: 'Fear, distance', 'Frustration, access', 'Excitement, closer'.

Think of an iceberg. The behavior is the tip we see. Fear, frustration, excitement and conflict sit below the waterline.

On screen: Text: “Emotion sits under the behavior” · The cards clear; an iceberg rises with Stare, Growl, Lunge, Bite on its tip above a wavy waterline, and Fear, Frustration, Excitement, Conflict below it.

But one emotion doesn't always make one behavior. One fearful dog freezes. Another runs. Another barks and lunges. And the same behavior can come from different emotions.

On screen: The iceberg shrinks aside and two lines type on: 'Same emotion, different behaviors' and 'Same behavior, different emotions'.

So the behavior alone isn't an emotional diagnosis. We look at the whole picture.

On screen: Text: “Look at the whole picture” · A green frame draws around everything.

ch01s14: Why this response?

Once we've looked at the ABC and the function, we can ask: why might this dog respond this way? And right behind that, most owners ask, what did I do wrong?

The honest answer? It's almost never one thing, or one mistake. It works like a recipe: several ingredients mix together, and the same ingredients can cook up reactivity, aggression, or both.
