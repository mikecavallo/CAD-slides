Chapter 1: Aggression and Reactivity: Starting with the Basics

ch01s01: Aggression and reactivity

Let's start with two words you'll hear a lot: aggression and reactivity. They're not the same thing, and they can overlap.

On screen: Text: “Aggression and reactivity” · The heading 'Aggression and reactivity' writes on in green; the snarling dog photo and the barking white dog photo slide in as two large circles side by side, labeled 'Aggression' and 'Reactivity'.

Aggression is behavior directed toward another individual or animal with an intent to threaten, push away, control, protect, or cause harm. It can show up in very different emotional situations.

On screen: Under the aggression circle, five small chips pop in one per word: Threaten, Push away, Control, Protect, Harm. A sixth line appears: 'Behavior with intent.'

Reactivity is an intense, over-the-top response to something in the environment. Think of a smoke alarm that screams every time you make toast.

On screen: Under the reactivity circle, a smoke alarm icon appears beside a tiny toast icon; red sound rings swell huge while the toast stays small.

A reactive response can include aggressive behavior, but it doesn't have to. And aggressive behavior alone doesn't tell us what the dog is feeling, or exactly what the behavior is accomplishing.

On screen: Text: “They overlap, but they're not the same” · The two circles slide together into a Venn diagram and the overlap tints light green.

That's why the label is only the starting point.

On screen: Text: “The label is only the starting point” · A 'Label' tag lands on the overlap and a green arrow runs from it to a card: 'The starting point, not the whole story'.

ch01s02: Look beyond the label

A dog may bark, growl, lunge, snap, freeze, move away, or bite. These behaviors matter, but the behavior alone does not tell us why the dog is responding that way.

On screen: Text: “Look beyond the label” · The heading 'Look beyond the label' writes on in green and seven behavior chips pop in a row, one per word: Bark, Growl, Lunge, Snap, Freeze, Move away, Bite.

Take barking. One dog may bark at a person because it wants the person to come closer. Another dog may bark because it wants the person to go away. Another may bark because it wants access to something the person has. And another may bark because the situation has become too overwhelming.

On screen: Text: “The same behavior can have different reasons” · A single large 'BARK' chip sits at center and four simple situations appear around it, one per sentence: Person at window, 'Come closer'; Person approaching, 'Go away'; Person holding toy, 'Give me that'; Overactive environment, 'Too much'.

The behavior is the same, but what is happening in the situation is different. And that difference matters when we decide how to help the dog.

On screen: Text: “Same behavior. Different context.” · The four examples remain on screen while the central 'BARK' chip stays unchanged; the four situations glow in turn.

So what influences how a dog responds in the first place? There are several factors that can contribute to behavior.

On screen: Text: “What contributes to behavior?” · The four examples fade out and several question marks appear around a dog icon.

ch01s03: Why this response?

When a dog's behavior changes, many owners immediately ask, “What did I do wrong?”

On screen: Text: “Why this response?” · The Lab and Pyrenees photo slides in as a portrait card at left and the heading 'Why this response?' writes on in green.

The honest answer? It's usually not one thing. A dog's behavior can be influenced by many underlying factors, which can contribute to reactivity, aggression, or both.

On screen: Text: “Usually a mix” · The photo slides away, the heading moves up, and eight blank ingredient chips appear in an arc on the right.

Think of it like a recipe. There are usually several ingredients behind the behavior we see.

On screen: Text: “Several factors contribute” · A large mixing bowl appears center right beneath the eight blank chips, which hover above it.

ch01s04: Nature and genetics

Ingredient one, genetics. Some dogs are simply born more sensitive, with a more easily triggered smoke alarm from day one.

On screen: Text: “Some dogs are born more sensitive” · The first chip, a DNA icon labeled 'Genetics and temperament', drops into the bowl with a small splash, and a tiny smoke alarm icon blinks beside it.

And a puppy's starting point can be influenced before it is even born. The mother's health and nutrition during pregnancy, along with significant stress during pregnancy, can influence fetal development. These early influences can become part of the foundation a puppy starts life with.

On screen: Text: “Before birth matters too” · The DNA chip stays in the bowl as a second chip labeled 'Prenatal environment' drops in. Simple icons appear around it, one per phrase: a pregnant dog (health), a food bowl (nutrition), and a stress symbol (stress).

Breed traits matter too. Dogs have been selectively bred for generations to perform specific jobs. Herders were bred to notice and control movement, terriers to hunt and pursue small animals, and guardian breeds to monitor and respond to potential threats. When you're looking at a dog or a mix, it's important to consider what those breeds were developed to do.

On screen: Text: “What was this dog bred to do?” · The Lab and Pyrenees photo slides in as a small portrait card beside the bowl; tags appear one per phrase: 'Herders: control movement', 'Terriers: hunt and pursue', 'Guardians: monitor and respond to threats'.

Those breed traits are tendencies, not guarantees. Individual dogs can vary, even within the same breed or mix.

On screen: Text: “Breed gives us clues” · Several small dog icons of different sizes and poses appear beneath each breed tag, showing variety within each type.

These are some of the things that can shape a dog's starting point before we ever meet them.

On screen: Text: “Every dog starts somewhere” · The photo and tags clear, the 'Breed traits' chip drops into the bowl, and the Genetics and temperament, Prenatal environment and Breed traits chips settle together with a soft glow.

ch01s05: Early life and socialization

Ingredient two, socialization. Early experiences help puppies learn what is familiar, safe, and normal. There is an important developmental period during the first few months when puppies are especially influenced by their experiences.

On screen: Text: “Early experiences matter” · The 'Socialization' chip lights up above the bowl, and a timeline appears beside it with an early developmental window highlighted and labeled 'First few months'.

Too little positive, appropriate exposure during this period can leave unfamiliar people, animals, places, or experiences harder for a dog to accept later. And if you adopt an older dog, some of that early history happened before you ever met them. That can make things harder to understand, but it doesn't mean behavior cannot change.

On screen: Text: “Too little exposure” · Gaps appear in the highlighted window, and example tags appear along the timeline: Strangers, Buses, Skateboards, New places.

But socialization can also go too far in the other direction. More exposure is not always better.

On screen: Text: “More is not always better” · The tags clear; the highlighted window expands and fills with overlapping icons of people, dogs, hands and places.

Think about the game "pass the puppy." A well-meaning group of people may take turns picking up a puppy, holding them, petting them, and passing them from person to person. The puppy may have very little choice about what is happening or when they get a break.

On screen: Text: “Pass the puppy” · The timeline clears; a puppy icon moves from one person icon to the next around a circle, with hands reaching toward it. A small 'Choice' meter stays near empty.

If a puppy repeatedly experiences new people, dogs, handling, or environments without enough opportunity to pause, move away, or choose whether to interact, those experiences can become overwhelming rather than helpful.

On screen: Text: “Too much, too fast” · More people, dog, hand and place icons crowd in around the puppy; the puppy icon shrinks as the activity grows.

And then there is the puppy who learned that every dog or person means a party. Running up to meet dog after dog, person after person can make seeing another dog extremely exciting, until the leash prevents the puppy from getting there. Hello, frustration. If that was your puppy, you were probably following common advice.

On screen: Text: “Every dog means a party” · The crowd clears; a row of happy dog icons appears with party sparkles, then a leash pulls tight between the puppy and the next dog and a small red label appears: 'Frustration'.

Early experiences are only one part of the picture, but they can become an important part of a dog's history.

On screen: Text: “Early experiences become part of the history” · The party row clears and the 'Socialization' chip drops into the bowl beside the earlier chips.

ch01s06: Age

Ingredient three, age. Adolescence runs from about six to eighteen months, up to two years in some big breeds. Think teenager with fur: big feelings, less self-control.

On screen: Text: “Adolescence: about 6 to 18 months” · On the first sentence the 'Age' chip drops in and the bowl shifts left; on 'Adolescence runs', a life-stage ruler draws beneath it with a shaded band labeled 'Adolescence, 6 to 18 months'.

Then comes social maturity, when your dog becomes an adult, between about one and three years. This is when reactivity or aggression often shows up or gets louder.

On screen: Text: “Social maturity: about 1 to 3 years” · A second band shades in, 'Social maturity, 1 to 3 years', and pulses as a small volume icon turns up above it.

Many dogs also get pickier about other dogs as they grow up. It's like a teenager who wants to go to parties and make a ton of friends. As an adult, they'd rather skip the party and see a few close friends.

On screen: Text: “More selective with age” · Two small cards appear under the ruler: 'Teen' with a party-popper icon and a crowd of little dots, then 'Adult' with a coffee icon and three dots, under the label 'More selective with age'.

That's normal, not a problem. But the dog who loved the dog park at one may not want it at three, and pushing them can end in a scuffle.

On screen: Text: “Normal, not a problem” · A small dog park card appears with a trees icon: 'Age 1: loved it' with a green check, then 'Age 3: not anymore' with an amber caution badge.

Senior dogs change too. Sore joints, fading senses, or changes in memory can affect how much they want to deal with the little things they used to tolerate. A dog who once ignored being bumped, crowded, startled, or bothered may have less patience for those interactions as they get older.

On screen: Text: “Senior dogs change too” · The cards clear; an older dog appears with small icons for sore joints, fading vision, hearing and memory. Around the dog, everyday interactions appear one per word: Bumped, Crowded, Startled, Bothered. A 'Tolerance' meter gradually lowers.

ch01s07: Past experiences

Ingredient four, past experiences. A single event can be enough: if an off-leash dog rushes yours once, they may never forget it.

On screen: Text: “One event can be enough” · The 'Past experiences' chip drops in and a lightning bolt strikes a single point on a short timeline labeled 'One event'.

Repeated experiences can shape behavior too. Imagine someone repeatedly takes an item away from your dog. Your dog may start reacting when that person approaches while they have something.

On screen: Text: “Repeated experiences shape responses” · A person icon approaches a dog icon with a toy and takes the toy away. The sequence repeats three times, and each time the dog's warning marks grow bigger as the person approaches.

And the more a dog practices a response, the easier that response can become. Practice makes patterns stronger. A dog that repeatedly barks, lunges, freezes, or snaps in a particular situation may begin to do it sooner and more automatically the next time.

On screen: Text: “Practice makes patterns stronger” · Three rows show the same encounter repeated; in each row the red response marker lands earlier along the row, with labels '1st', '5th', '20th'.

It's like a path across a lawn: walk it every day and it wears in, until it's the way you go without thinking. The good news is we can build a new path.

On screen: Text: “Every rehearsal deepens the path” · The rows fade into a faint dotted line across a green lawn that thickens into a worn path, and on 'the good news' a fresh green dotted path sketches in beside it.

ch01s08: Training tools and methods

Ingredient five, training tools and methods. The tools and techniques we use can also affect behavior. Research links harsh methods with more fear, stress, and aggression. Think leash jerks, prong, choke or shock collars, and pinning a dog down.

On screen: Text: “Training can affect behavior too” · The 'Training tools and methods' chip drops in and a short row of red-outlined tags appears beside it, one per phrase: Leash pops, Prong and choke collars, Shock collars, Alpha rolls.

And when a dog reacts to something in their environment, it is not because they're trying to be the boss. Think about how much of your dog's life you already control. You decide where they sleep, when they go outside, where they go, how long they stay there, when they eat, and when they get access to the things they need and enjoy.

On screen: Text: “You already control most of their world” · The tags clear; a dog icon sits at center and icons appear around it one per phrase: Bed, Door, Walk, Time, Food, Toys, Outside. Each connects to a small 'Human decides' label.

A dog reacting to something in their environment isn't trying to take over the household or establish rank. They're responding to something happening in that moment, not trying to change their place in the family.

On screen: Text: “A reaction isn't a power struggle” · The life icons fade; a crown labeled 'Boss?' appears over the dog and fades away. The dog stays, facing a small trigger icon in front of them.

The idea that dogs need to be dominated has led to tools and techniques designed to suppress behavior through force or intimidation. But suppressing behavior isn't the same as understanding why a dog is responding that way and helping them change that response.

On screen: Text: “Suppressing behavior ≠ changing behavior” · 'Suppress' appears in red over the dog icon and fades away; the words 'Understand' and 'Help' replace it in green.

If you've used any of these, be kind to yourself. You did your best, and now you know more. A trainer who works without force can help you switch safely to a comfortable harness.

On screen: Text: “Be kind to yourself” · Everything fades as a green heart icon pulses once over the bowl.

ch01s09: Pain and discomfort

Ingredient six, pain and discomfort. A dog who is hurting may respond differently to things they previously tolerated. Pain can lower a dog's tolerance for being touched, bumped, approached, handled, or for things happening around them in their environment.

On screen: Text: “Pain can change behavior” · The 'Pain and discomfort' chip drops into the bowl. A dog appears with small indicators at different areas of the body, then icons pop in one per word: Touch, Bumping, Approach, Handling, Noise, Movement.

And pain isn't always obvious. Dogs don't always limp, cry, or show us exactly where something hurts. A change in behavior may sometimes be one of the first clues that something isn't right.

On screen: Text: “Pain isn't always obvious” · The icons clear; the dog stands normally while faint question marks appear around them. A second panel shows the same dog leaning away from a hand reaching toward them.

That's why a change in behavior, especially a sudden change, is worth taking seriously. Before assuming a behavior problem is purely behavioral, we need to consider whether pain or another physical problem could be contributing.

On screen: Text: “Behavior change can be a clue” · The panels clear; a sudden-change arrow appears beside a dog and branches into two paths: 'Behavior' and 'Physical health'.

ch01s10: Putting it together

By now, you can see that a dog's behavior usually isn't explained by one thing. Genetics, early life, age, pain and discomfort, past experiences, and the methods used with the dog can all become part of the picture.

On screen: Text: “Behavior is shaped by many factors” · The mixing bowl returns to center screen with all eight blank chips above it. The chips drop in as they are named: Genetics and temperament, Prenatal environment and Breed traits on 'Genetics', Socialization on 'early life', Age, Pain and discomfort, Past experiences, and Training tools and methods on 'the methods'.

Aggression and reactivity describe what we see, but they don't explain why the dog is responding that way. A dog can display aggressive behavior without being an aggressive dog.
