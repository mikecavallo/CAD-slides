Chapter 1: Aggression and reactivity

ch01s01: What is aggression?

Let's start with the word that scares owners most: aggression.

On screen: Text: “What is aggression?” · the snarling dog photo slides in as a portrait card on the left and the heading 'What is aggression?' writes on in green at right.

Aggression isn't a label for who your dog is. It describes what a behavior is for, usually one of four jobs.

On screen: Text: “Behavior aimed at a goal” · The accent bar wipes in under the heading and four empty card outlines appear in a two-by-two grid.

Job one, create distance. Get away from me, or make that thing go away. Job two, stop a perceived threat.

On screen: The top row fills: arrows pointing apart labeled 'Create distance', and a shield labeled 'Stop a threat'.

Notice that word, perceived. The threat only has to feel dangerous to your dog.

On screen: Text: “It only has to feel threatening” · The shield card pulses and a small eye badge reading 'Perceived' pins to its corner.

Job three, guard something they value, like a bone or the couch. Job four, change how things turn out, like growling so the brushing stops.

On screen: Text: “Four jobs aggression does” · The bottom row fills: a bone icon labeled 'Guard what they value', and a hand icon labeled 'Change the outcome'.

What makes it aggression? Your dog is actively trying to make something happen. That's behavior with intent: not spite, just a goal.

On screen: Text: “Behavior with intent.” · 'Behavior with intent.' lands large in green over the accent bar beneath the four job cards.

ch01s02: What is reactivity?

Next, reactivity. It's an over-the-top emotional reaction to something in your dog's world, way bigger than the moment calls for.

On screen: Text: “What is reactivity?” · the barking white dog photo slides in on the right, the heading 'What is reactivity?' writes on at left, and the definition fades in beneath it with 'over-the-top' highlighted in green.

Picture a smoke alarm that screams every time you make toast. The alarm isn't broken. It's set way too sensitive.

On screen: Text: “A smoke alarm for toast” · A smoke alarm icon appears beside a tiny slice of toast; red sound rings blast out of the alarm and swell huge while the toast stays small.

ch01s03: Magnitude, not intent

Here's the big idea. Think of reactivity as the volume on that smoke alarm. It tells you how loud, not why.

Take two dogs barking at the same mail carrier, both at max. One wants the mail carrier gone. The other is desperate to say hi.

On screen: Text: “Same volume, different reasons” · The knob splits into two identical knobs, both pinned at max, with a mail icon between them and small labels beneath: 'Go away' and 'Come say hi'.

Same volume, different reason. And the reason is where the useful information is.

On screen: Text: “The reason matters” · The two knobs glide back together into one, and a green question mark pops above it.

ch01s04: Often a bit of both

So is your dog reactive or aggressive? Honestly, most dogs don't fit neatly in one box. There's a lot of overlap.

On screen: Text: “Reactive or aggressive?” · The split screen builds: the barking white dog photo on the left, the snarling dog photo on the right, the heading 'Reactive or aggressive?' above, and a thin green divider between them.

A reactive dog might be saying, this is too much, or I want this now.

On screen: Two speech bubbles pop up from the reactivity photo, one per phrase: 'This is too much' and 'I want this now'.

A dog using aggression might be saying, I will make this stop, or I will make this happen.

On screen: Two speech bubbles pop up from the aggression photo, one per phrase: 'I will make this stop' and 'I will make this happen'.

But the same bark, lunge or growl can carry any of those messages, and one dog can say all four in a single week.

On screen: Text: “Often a bit of both” · Both photos shrink into circles and slide together into a Venn diagram; all four bubbles drift into the overlap, which tints light green.

So instead of chasing a label, I ask two better questions. What is this behavior doing for your dog? That's its function. And what's driving it? Those are the underlying factors.

Put those answers together, and they tell us which steps your dog needs. That's what the rest of this lesson is about.

ch01s05: Behavior is communication

When someone stands too close in line, you don't shove them. You shift, give them a look, maybe say excuse me.

On screen: The plan card clears; two person icons stand side by side, one inching closer, and a speech bubble pops up from the other reading 'Excuse me.'

Dogs do the same. A hard stare, a growl, a snap: each says back off, go away, or this is mine. Even a bite is a message, just the loudest.

On screen: Text: “Back off. Go away. This is mine.” · The person icons swap for a dog icon and three green speech bubbles pop in around it, one by one: 'Back off', 'Go away', 'This is mine'.

Every one of those messages is driven by emotion: fear, sometimes frustration, sometimes a strong urge to guard. The behavior is just the part we see.

On screen: Text: “Emotion drives the behavior” · The bubbles shrink into four labels on an iceberg tip, Stare, Growl, Snap, Bite, above a wavy waterline; below it, Fear, Frustration and Guarding rise into view.

ch01s06: The warning ladder

Luckily, most dogs don't go straight to a bite. They climb a ladder of warnings first, though a startled or hurting dog can race up it in a split second.

The bottom rungs are quiet: a lip lick, a yawn that doesn't fit the moment, a head turned away. Then the body stiffens and the stare goes hard.

If nobody listens, it gets louder. A growl, a lip lifted to show teeth, then a snap, often at the air. Even here, your dog is holding back.

On screen: Text: “Growl. Snarl. Snap.” · The next three rungs fill one at a time, shifting from yellow to orange: 'Growl', 'Snarl, lip lift', 'Snap'.

And only at the very top, after all of that, comes a bite.

Every rung below the bite is your dog trying not to bite. Those warnings are a gift.

On screen: Text: “Warnings are a gift” · A green bracket wraps the lower five rungs and a gift icon pops in beside it.

Barking and lunging can be loud warnings too. This week, watch for the quiet rungs first, like a lip lick, a look away or a stiff body, and give your dog space.

ch01s07: Three directions

Remember, the reason behind the behavior is where the useful information is. For a lot of dogs, the feeling pulls in one of three directions.

On screen: Text: “Three directions” · A round dog badge appears at center and three unlabeled arrows fan out from it: left, right and up.

Direction one is away: your dog is scared and wants distance. A skateboard rattles by, and they bark like their life depends on it. To them, it kind of does.

On screen: Text: “Away: fear” · The left arrow from the dog badge lights up with an alert-triangle icon and the label 'Away: fear'.

Direction two is toward. Your dog wants something so badly it hurts, like the dog across the street or a squirrel. And the leash says no.

On screen: Text: “Toward: frustration” · The right arrow lights up with a magnet icon, a leash line pulls taut along it, and the label reads 'Toward: frustration'.

That's road rage. You can see exactly where you want to go, and you're stuck in traffic. Sooner or later, you lean on the horn.

On screen: Text: “Leash road rage” · A car icon slides onto the right arrow with little horn sound lines bursting from it.

Direction three is release: built-up tension or excitement bursting out, and sometimes it's even fun. Think of the dog who spins and screams when you grab the leash.

On screen: Text: “Release: built-up tension” · The upward arrow bursts out with a zap icon and the label 'Release: built-up tension'.

One catch: scared dogs often lunge toward what they fear, to drive it off. So don't judge by the pull. Ask what they're trying to get.

On screen: Text: “Watch the goal, not the pull” · The Away arrow's tip briefly flips to point forward with a small caution badge flashing on it, then snaps back.

Lots of dogs mix all three. Next time yours goes off, ask: away, toward, or release? Guessing is fine.

On screen: Text: “Away, toward, or release?” · All three arrows glow together, a question card slides up beneath the dog badge, and a small green 'Try this' badge with a notebook-pen icon pins to the top left corner.

ch01s08: Bred for a job

One more piece of the puzzle: the job your dog was bred for. Herders were made to chase and control movement. Terriers, to hunt. Hounds, to follow their nose. Guardians, to watch over their home.

On screen: Text: “Bred for a job” · The heading 'Bred for a job' writes on and four breed cards pop in one per phrase: 'Herders: chase and control movement', 'Terriers: hunt', 'Hounds: follow their nose', 'Guardians: watch over home'.

Those drives are normal, and they don't switch off. When they don't get a healthy outlet, the pressure builds, and a bike, a squirrel or the mail carrier becomes the target.

On screen: Text: “No outlet, more pressure” · The cards shrink to one side; a pressure meter fills to red and a zap arcs from it to three small targets: bike, squirrel, mail.

So give the drive a job: flirt poles for chasers, sniffing games for noses, tug for hunters. It won't fix reactivity on its own, but it takes a lot of pressure off.

On screen: Text: “Give the drive a job” · The meter drains to green as three outlet chips pop in, 'Flirt pole', 'Sniff games', 'Tug', under a green label 'Give the drive a job'.

Chapter 2: Why reactivity and aggression happen

ch02s01: Why reactivity and aggression happen

The question I hear most is, why is my dog like this? Why the barking and lunging, or the growling and snapping? And right behind it, what did I do wrong?

On screen: Text: “Why is my dog like this?” · the Lab and Pyrenees photo slides in as a portrait card at left and the heading 'Why reactivity and aggression happen' writes on in green.

The honest answer? It's almost never one thing, or one mistake. These are the underlying factors, and they work like a recipe: the same ingredients can cook up reactivity, aggression, or both. Here are seven common ones.

On screen: Text: “Usually a mix” · A large mixing bowl appears center right with seven blank ingredient chips hovering above it, plus a smaller dotted chip marked with a plus sign.

ch02s02: Nature and early life

Ingredient one, genetics. Some dogs are simply born more sensitive, with a touchier smoke alarm from day one.

On screen: Text: “Some dogs are born sensitive” · The first chip, a DNA icon labeled 'Genetics and temperament', drops into the bowl with a small splash, and a tiny smoke alarm icon blinks beside it.

Breed traits nudge things too. Remember those jobs? A guardian bred to be wary of strangers may growl at visitors, and a herder may nip at running kids. Breed isn't destiny, but it loads the dice.

On screen: Text: “Breed loads the dice” · the Lab and Pyrenees photo slides back in as a small portrait card beside the bowl; the tag 'Guardians: wary of strangers' points to the Great Pyrenees, and 'Herders: chase and nip' pops in beside it as a text tag.

Ingredient two, socialization. In roughly the first three months, a puppy's brain sorts the world into safe and scary. What they meet calmly in that window tends to feel normal later.

On screen: Text: “Roughly the first three months” · The photo card slides away, the 'Socialization' chip drops in, and a timeline bar draws beside the bowl with a green window lit over its start, labeled 'Roughly the first three months'.

Miss that window, and buses, strangers and skateboards can stay harder to file as normal. If you adopted an older dog, it closed before you met. Harder, not hopeless.

On screen: Text: “Too little” · Gaps appear in the green window and a label 'Too little' drops in beneath it with a closed-door icon.

Or the other way: too much, too fast, and a puppy learns the world is overwhelming.

On screen: Text: “Too much, too fast” · A second label 'Too much, too fast' drops in with an alert-triangle icon.

And a puppy who greeted every dog learns every dog means a party, until the leash says no. Hello, frustration. If that was your puppy, you followed common advice.

On screen: Text: “Everyone is a party” · A third label 'Everyone is a party' drops in with a party-popper icon, and a small leash line pulls tight beneath it.

ch02s03: Age

Ingredient three, age. Adolescence runs from about six to eighteen months, up to two years in some big breeds. Think teenager with fur: big feelings, less self-control.

On screen: Text: “Adolescence: about 6 to 18 months” · On the first sentence the 'Age' chip drops in and the bowl shifts left; on 'Adolescence runs', a life-stage ruler draws beneath it with a shaded band labeled 'Adolescence, 6 to 18 months'.

Then comes social maturity, when your dog becomes an adult, between about one and three years. This is when reactivity or aggression often shows up or gets louder.

On screen: Text: “Social maturity: about 1 to 3 years” · A second band shades in, 'Social maturity, 1 to 3 years', and pulses as a small volume icon turns up above it.

Many dogs also get pickier about other dogs as they grow up. It's like a teenager who wants to hit the club and make a ton of friends. As an adult, they'd rather skip the club and see a few close friends.

On screen: Text: “More selective with age” · Two small cards appear under the ruler: 'Teen' with a party-popper icon and a crowd of little dots, then 'Adult' with a coffee icon and three dots, under the label 'More selective with age'.

That's normal, not a problem. But the dog who loved the dog park at one may not want it at three, and pushing them can end in a scuffle.

On screen: Text: “Normal, not a problem” · A small dog park card appears with a trees icon: 'Age 1: loved it' with a green check, then 'Age 3: not anymore' with an amber caution badge.

Senior dogs change too. Sore joints lower tolerance, and fading senses or a foggy memory make the world less predictable, and less safe.

On screen: Text: “Senior changes” · A final marker lights at the far end of the ruler with a clock icon: 'Senior'.

ch02s04: Past experiences and tools

Ingredient four, past experiences. One scary moment can be enough: if an off-leash dog rushes yours once, they may never forget it. Fear learns fast.

On screen: Text: “One bad moment can be enough” · The 'Past experiences' chip drops in and a lightning bolt strikes a single point on a short timeline labeled 'One bad moment'.

Ingredient five, training tools. Research links harsh methods with more fear, stress and aggression. Think leash jerks, prong, choke or shock collars, and pinning a dog down.

On screen: Text: “Harsh tools, more fear” · The 'Training tools' chip drops in and a short row of red-outlined tags appears beside it: Leash pops, Prong and choke collars, Shock collars, Alpha rolls.

If your dog feels pain whenever another dog appears, they learn other dogs predict pain. The fear grows, even if the barking stops for a while.

On screen: Text: “Other dogs predict pain” · The tags are replaced by an icon equation: dog icon plus zap icon equals alert-triangle icon, labeled 'Other dogs predict pain'.

Those tools often come from the outdated idea that your dog wants to be the boss. Dogs who react or aggress aren't after rank. They're scared, frustrated, overwhelmed, or protecting something they value.

On screen: Text: “Not dominance. Big feelings.” · The equation clears; a small crown icon labeled 'Boss?' appears over the bowl, a red X crosses it out, and four soft tags replace it: Scared, Frustrated, Overwhelmed, Protecting.

If you've used any of these, be kind to yourself. You did your best, and now you know more. A trainer who works without force can help you switch safely to a comfortable harness.

On screen: Text: “Be kind to yourself” · The crown and tags fade as a green heart icon pulses once over the bowl.

ch02s05: Frustration, pain and the mix

Ingredient six, barriers. Remember road rage? A leash, fence or window is the traffic jam: your dog sees what they want, can't reach it, and the pressure climbs. It's how a lot of fence fighting starts.

On screen: Text: “Held back by a leash or barrier” · The 'Leash and barrier frustration' chip drops in and the dog-behind-the-fence photo slides in at the left edge, the dog peering through the iron fence.

Ingredient seven, pain, and don't skip this one. Think how short your fuse gets with a headache. Dogs feel that too, and hide it well.

Pain is common in dogs with behavior problems, and it's a big reason behind sudden growling or snapping. So every dog with reactivity or aggression needs a vet check, especially after a sudden change.

On screen: Text: “Rule out pain first” · A green badge reads 'Vet check for every dog', then a question ticks in beneath it, 'Sudden change?', with an arrow marked 'See the vet first'.

And there are many other factors, which we'll get into later in the course.

On screen: Text: “Many other factors” · The dotted plus chip fills with 'Many other factors' and drops in, and the whole bowl glows.

Please hear this. Most of this list was never in your control, and none of it is your fault. But understanding it is now in your hands.

On screen: Text: “It's not your fault.” · 'It's not your fault.' fades up large beneath the glowing bowl with a small green heart icon.

ch03s03: Warnings and frustration

Three, the warnings stop working. If nobody listens to a growl, or someone punishes it, your dog learns the polite rungs are pointless.

So next time, they may skip the warnings and go straight to the top.

On screen: Text: “The warnings get skipped” · The lower rungs fade to grey and a red arrow jumps from the bottom rung straight to 'Bite'.

Four, frustration boils over. A dog who's held back and wound up can whip around and snap at whatever's closest, even your hand.

ch03s04: Never punish a growl

Which brings me to the most important rule here: never punish a growl. If you have, you're in good company. Most of us were taught to.

On screen: Text: “Never punish a growl.” · Everything clears and 'Never punish a growl.' lands large in green at the center of the swirl.

Punishing a growl is like taping over your car's check engine light. The light's gone, but the engine problem isn't. You've just lost your warning.

Punishment can stop the growl, but not the fear underneath, and often makes the fear worse. That's how you get a dog who seems to bite out of nowhere.

Chapter 5: What is a trigger?

ch05s01: What is a trigger?

The dog on the corner, the skateboard, the mail carrier: these are triggers. A trigger is anything that raises your dog's stress, whether they react out loud, quietly, or not at all.

On screen: Text: “Anything that raises stress” · The heading 'What is a trigger?' writes on, the trigger word cloud fades in as a portrait card on the right, and the definition lands in the accent bar.

That last part matters. A dog who freezes and stares can be just as stressed as a barking dog. Sometimes more.

On screen: Text: “Quiet can still be stressed” · The word cloud dims; two thermometer icons appear side by side, labeled 'Barking' and 'Frozen stare', both filling to the same high red level.

ch05s02: Feeling first

Inside your dog, it goes like this. The trigger causes an emotion first, and that emotion drives the reaction.

On screen: Text: “Trigger. Emotion. Reaction.” · Three nodes draw left to right with arrows between them: alert-triangle 'Trigger', heart 'Emotion', volume 'Reaction'.

You know that jolt when a friend jumps out and yells boo? Your heart leaps before you know it's them. Feeling first, scream second.

On screen: Text: “Feeling first, then reaction” · The Emotion node pulses large with a zap icon, and the Reaction node lights up a beat later.

That's why we work on the feeling, not just the barking. Change the feeling, and the reaction changes with it.

On screen: Text: “Change the feeling” · A green ring circles the Emotion node with the label 'This is where we work'.

ch05s03: Every dog's list

Every dog has their own list. Some are fine with every person on the planet, until one is wearing a hat.

ch05s04: Why just exposing them backfires

Maybe someone told you to keep exposing your dog to the scary thing until they get over it. That can make things worse.

On screen: Text: “Just expose them?” · A dog icon appears in the center and bike, car, bell, volume and users icons crowd in around it from every side under the words 'Just expose them?'

If you're scared of spiders, a night in a room full of them won't make you love them. You're just surviving. That's flooding.

On screen: Text: “Flooding: just surviving it” · The label 'Flooding' stamps in red over the crowded cluster.

Flooding can cause sensitization, where fear grows with every exposure. Daily walks that get too close can do it too. Or a dog shuts down and goes quiet, which looks calm but isn't.

A dog only gets used to something when the dose is small enough to feel safe. Far enough away, quiet enough, and short enough.
