## Aggression and Reactivity: Starting with the Basics

Let's start with two words you'll hear a lot: aggression and reactivity. They're not the same thing, and they can overlap. Aggression is behavior intended to threaten, push away, control, protect, or cause harm. It can show up in very different situations. Reactivity is an intense, over-the-top response to something in the environment. Think of a smoke alarm that screams every time you make toast. A reactive response can include aggressive behavior, but it doesn't have to. And aggressive behavior alone doesn't tell us what the dog is feeling or exactly what the behavior is accomplishing. That's why the label is only the starting point.

A dog may bark, growl, lunge, snap, freeze, cower, move away, or bite. These behaviors matter, but the behavior alone does not tell us why the dog is responding that way. Take barking. One dog may bark at a person because it wants the person to come closer. Another dog may bark because it wants the person to go away. Another may bark because it wants access to something the person has. And another may bark because the situation has become too overwhelming. The behavior is the same, but why it's happening in each situation is different. And that difference matters when we decide how to help the dog. So what influences how a dog responds in the first place? There are many factors that contribute to behavior. In this video, we'll take a closer look at some of the influences that shape how a dog responds.

When a dog's behavior changes, one of the first questions an owner may ask is, "Why is my dog acting this way?" The answer? It's not just one reason. A dog's behavior is influenced by many factors, which can contribute to fearful, reactive, aggressive, or other responses. Think of it like a recipe. There are several ingredients that can contribute to the behavior we see.

Ingredient one: genetics. Dogs are born with different temperaments and personality traits. Some are naturally more sensitive to their surroundings, while others may be more easygoing or less reactive to change. Some dogs simply start life with a more sensitive "smoke alarm," meaning their nervous system may be more easily triggered from the beginning.

Ingredient two: prenatal environment. A puppy's starting point can be influenced before it is even born. The mother's health, nutrition, and experiences during pregnancy can influence fetal development. These early influences can become part of the foundation a puppy starts life with.

Ingredient three: breed history and purpose. Dogs have been selectively bred for generations to perform specific jobs. The American Kennel Club sorts many breeds into groups based on those jobs, and they can give us clues about behaviors a dog may be naturally inclined toward. Those original jobs can influence the kinds of things a dog notices, how they respond to movement or their environment, and what behaviors they may be more likely to offer. For example, sporting dogs were bred to assist hunters by finding, flushing, or retrieving game, like Golden Retrievers, Labrador Retrievers, Pointers, and Spaniels. Depending on the breed, that can mean being especially attentive to movement, searching, carrying objects, or working closely with a person. Hounds were bred to pursue game, using either sight or scent, like Beagles, Bloodhounds, Greyhounds, and Dachshunds. A hound may be especially motivated to chase something fast or follow a scent that has captured their attention. Working dogs were bred for jobs such as guarding property, pulling, rescue, and other working tasks, like Rottweilers, Great Danes, Siberian Huskies, and Newfoundlands. Those jobs can come with tendencies such as persistence, physical engagement, or close attention to unfamiliar people and activity around their home. Terriers were bred primarily to hunt and eliminate vermin and other small animals, like Parson Russell Terriers, Airedale Terriers, and Miniature Schnauzers. Some may be particularly interested in quick movement from squirrels, cats, or other small animals. Toy breeds are small companion dogs, bred primarily for companionship, like Chihuahuas, Pomeranians, Maltese, and Yorkshire Terriers. Many are closely tuned in to their people, and because they're small, being picked up, loomed over, or crowded can feel like a much bigger deal. And herding dogs were bred to control and move livestock, like German Shepherd Dogs, Border Collies, Australian Shepherds, and Corgis. Some may be especially tuned in to things that move and may naturally want to follow, control, or redirect that movement. That doesn't mean a dog will automatically behave a certain way because of its breed. Breed history gives us clues about tendencies, not guarantees. Individual dogs can vary considerably, even within the same breed or mix. But when we're trying to understand a dog's behavior, knowing what those breeds were originally developed to do can give us another piece of the puzzle.

Ingredient four, socialization. Early experiences help puppies learn what is familiar, safe, and normal. There is an important developmental period during the first few months when puppies are especially influenced by what they encounter. When puppies don't have enough positive, appropriate exposure during this period, unfamiliar people, animals, places, or experiences may be harder for them to accept later. And if you adopt an older dog, some of what shaped their early expectations happened before you ever met them. But appropriate socialization isn't simply about exposing a puppy to as many things as possible. The way those experiences happen matters too. Puppies need opportunities to observe, approach, move away, and take a break. They should have some choice in whether and how they interact with something new. Think about the game "pass the puppy." A well-meaning group of people may take turns picking up a puppy, holding them, petting them, and passing them from person to person. The puppy may have very little choice about what is happening or when they get a break. Even when the people involved have good intentions, repeated handling without an opportunity to pause, move away, or choose whether to interact can make those experiences overwhelming rather than helpful. And then there is the puppy who learned that every dog or person means a party. Running up to meet dog after dog, person after person can make seeing another dog extremely exciting, until the leash prevents the puppy from getting there. Hello, frustration. If that was your puppy, you were probably following common advice. Socialization isn't about checking off a list of people, dogs, places, and experiences. It's about helping a puppy build positive, flexible associations while giving them appropriate support and choice. Socialization is one ingredient in the larger picture we need to consider when we're trying to understand behavior.

Ingredient five, age. Adolescence runs from about six to eighteen months, up to two years in some big breeds. Think teenager with fur: big feelings, less self-control. Then comes social maturity, when your dog becomes an adult, between about one and three years. This is when reactivity or aggression often shows up or gets louder. Many dogs also get pickier about other dogs as they grow up. It's like a teenager who wants to go to parties and make a ton of friends. As an adult, they'd rather skip the party and see a few close friends. That's normal, not a problem. But the dog who loved the dog park at one may not want it at three, and pushing them can end in a fight. Senior dogs change too. Sore joints, fading senses, or changes in memory can affect how much they want to deal with the little things they used to tolerate. A dog who once ignored being bumped, crowded, startled, or bothered may have less patience for those interactions as they get older.

Ingredient six, past experiences. A single event can be enough: if an off-leash dog rushes yours once, they may never forget it. Repeated experiences can shape behavior too. Imagine someone repeatedly takes an item away from your dog. Your dog may start reacting when that person approaches while they have something. And the more a dog practices a response, the easier that response can become. Practice makes patterns stronger. A dog that repeatedly barks, lunges, freezes, or snaps in a particular situation may begin to do it sooner and more automatically the next time. It's like a path across a lawn: walk it every day and it wears in, until it's the way you go without thinking. The good news is we can build a new path.

Ingredient seven, training tools and methods. The tools and techniques we use also affect behavior. Research links harsh methods with more fear, stress, and aggression. Think leash jerks, prong, choke or shock collars, and pinning a dog down. And when a dog reacts to something in their environment, it is not because they're trying to be the boss. Think about how much of your dog's life you already control. You decide where they sleep, when they go outside, where they go, how long they stay there, when they eat, and when they get access to the things they need and enjoy. A dog reacting to something in their environment isn't trying to take over the household or establish rank. They're responding to something happening in that moment, not trying to change their place in the family. The idea that dogs need to be dominated has led to tools and techniques designed to suppress behavior through force or intimidation. But suppressing behavior isn't the same as understanding why a dog is responding that way and helping them change that response. We will get into this much more later. If you've used any of these, be kind to yourself. You did your best, and now you know more. A trainer who works without force can help you switch safely to a comfortable harness.

Ingredient eight, pain and discomfort. A dog who is in pain can have a lower tolerance for their environment. And pain isn't always obvious. Dogs don't always limp, cry, or show us exactly where something hurts. A change in behavior may sometimes be one of the first clues that something isn't right. That's why a change in behavior, especially a sudden change, is worth taking seriously. A veterinarian can help determine whether pain, illness, or another underlying physical condition could be contributing to the behavior. Even when a physical condition is contributing, addressing the behavior itself is still important.

By now, you can see that a dog's behavior usually isn't explained by one thing. Genetics and temperament, prenatal environment, breed history and purpose, socialization, age, pain and discomfort, past experiences, and the methods used with the dog can all become ingredients in the behavior we see. But not all of those ingredients are visible on the surface. What we see is the behavior. And to understand it more fully, we need to look at what is happening around it. That's where we'll go next.
