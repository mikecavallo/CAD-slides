# Understanding Reactivity and Aggression, Part 1

## Aggression and Reactivity: Starting with the Basics

Let's start with two words you'll hear a lot: aggression and reactivity. They're not the same thing, and they can overlap. Aggression is behavior directed toward another individual or animal with an intent to threaten, push away, control, protect, or cause harm. It can show up in very different emotional situations. Reactivity is an intense, over-the-top response to something in the environment. Think of a smoke alarm that screams every time you make toast. A reactive response can include aggressive behavior, but it doesn't have to. And aggressive behavior alone doesn't tell us what the dog is feeling, or exactly what the behavior is accomplishing. That's why the label is only the starting point.

A dog may bark, growl, lunge, snap, freeze, move away, or bite. These behaviors matter, but the behavior alone does not tell us why the dog is responding that way. Take barking. One dog may bark at a person because it wants the person to come closer. Another dog may bark because it wants the person to go away. Another may bark because it wants access to something the person has. And another may bark because the situation has become too overwhelming. The behavior is the same, but what is happening in the situation is different. And that difference matters when we decide how to help the dog. So what influences how a dog responds in the first place? There are several factors that can contribute to behavior.

When a dog's behavior changes, many owners immediately ask, “What did I do wrong?” The honest answer? It's usually not one thing. A dog's behavior can be influenced by many underlying factors, which can contribute to reactivity, aggression, or both. Think of it like a recipe. There are usually several ingredients behind the behavior we see.

Ingredient one, genetics. Some dogs are simply born more sensitive, with a more easily triggered smoke alarm from day one. And a puppy's starting point can be influenced before it is even born. The mother's health and nutrition during pregnancy, along with significant stress during pregnancy, can influence fetal development. These early influences can become part of the foundation a puppy starts life with. Breed traits matter too. Dogs have been selectively bred for generations to perform specific jobs. Herders were bred to notice and control movement, terriers to hunt and pursue small animals, and guardian breeds to monitor and respond to potential threats. When you're looking at a dog or a mix, it's important to consider what those breeds were developed to do. Those breed traits are tendencies, not guarantees. Individual dogs can vary, even within the same breed or mix. These are some of the things that can shape a dog's starting point before we ever meet them.

Ingredient two, socialization. Early experiences help puppies learn what is familiar, safe, and normal. There is an important developmental period during the first few months when puppies are especially influenced by their experiences. Too little positive, appropriate exposure during this period can leave unfamiliar people, animals, places, or experiences harder for a dog to accept later. And if you adopt an older dog, some of that early history happened before you ever met them. That can make things harder to understand, but it doesn't mean behavior cannot change. But socialization can also go too far in the other direction. More exposure is not always better. Think about the game "pass the puppy." A well-meaning group of people may take turns picking up a puppy, holding them, petting them, and passing them from person to person. The puppy may have very little choice about what is happening or when they get a break. If a puppy repeatedly experiences new people, dogs, handling, or environments without enough opportunity to pause, move away, or choose whether to interact, those experiences can become overwhelming rather than helpful. And then there is the puppy who learned that every dog or person means a party. Running up to meet dog after dog, person after person can make seeing another dog extremely exciting, until the leash prevents the puppy from getting there. Hello, frustration. If that was your puppy, you were probably following common advice. Early experiences are only one part of the picture, but they can become an important part of a dog's history.

Ingredient three, age. Adolescence runs from about six to eighteen months, up to two years in some big breeds. Think teenager with fur: big feelings, less self-control. Then comes social maturity, when your dog becomes an adult, between about one and three years. This is when reactivity or aggression often shows up or gets louder. Many dogs also get pickier about other dogs as they grow up. It's like a teenager who wants to go to parties and make a ton of friends. As an adult, they'd rather skip the party and see a few close friends. That's normal, not a problem. But the dog who loved the dog park at one may not want it at three, and pushing them can end in a scuffle. Senior dogs change too. Sore joints, fading senses, or changes in memory can affect how much they want to deal with the little things they used to tolerate. A dog who once ignored being bumped, crowded, startled, or bothered may have less patience for those interactions as they get older.

Ingredient four, past experiences. A single event can be enough: if an off-leash dog rushes yours once, they may never forget it. Repeated experiences can shape behavior too. Imagine someone repeatedly takes an item away from your dog. Your dog may start reacting when that person approaches while they have something. And the more a dog practices a response, the easier that response can become. Practice makes patterns stronger. A dog that repeatedly barks, lunges, freezes, or snaps in a particular situation may begin to do it sooner and more automatically the next time. It's like a path across a lawn: walk it every day and it wears in, until it's the way you go without thinking. The good news is we can build a new path.

Ingredient five, training tools and methods. The tools and techniques we use can also affect behavior. Research links harsh methods with more fear, stress, and aggression. Think leash jerks, prong, choke or shock collars, and pinning a dog down. And when a dog reacts to something in their environment, it is not because they're trying to be the boss. Think about how much of your dog's life you already control. You decide where they sleep, when they go outside, where they go, how long they stay there, when they eat, and when they get access to the things they need and enjoy. A dog reacting to something in their environment isn't trying to take over the household or establish rank. They're responding to something happening in that moment, not trying to change their place in the family. The idea that dogs need to be dominated has led to tools and techniques designed to suppress behavior through force or intimidation. But suppressing behavior isn't the same as understanding why a dog is responding that way and helping them change that response. If you've used any of these, be kind to yourself. You did your best, and now you know more. A trainer who works without force can help you switch safely to a comfortable harness.

Ingredient six, pain and discomfort. A dog who is hurting may respond differently to things they previously tolerated. Pain can lower a dog's tolerance for being touched, bumped, approached, handled, or for things happening around them in their environment. And pain isn't always obvious. Dogs don't always limp, cry, or show us exactly where something hurts. A change in behavior may sometimes be one of the first clues that something isn't right. That's why a change in behavior, especially a sudden change, is worth taking seriously. Before assuming a behavior problem is purely behavioral, we need to consider whether pain or another physical problem could be contributing. That starts with a vet visit.

By now, you can see that a dog's behavior usually isn't explained by one thing. Genetics, early life, age, pain and discomfort, past experiences, and the methods used with the dog can all become part of the picture. Aggression and reactivity describe what we see, but they don't explain why the dog is responding that way. A dog can display aggressive behavior without being an aggressive dog. There is more to understanding behavior than what we see on the surface. In the next chapter, we'll look at what the behavior may be accomplishing, how that helps us understand what's happening for that individual dog, and how we can use that information to change the behavior.
